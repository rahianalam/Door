To buy the right door knob or handle, you need four facts about your door: its function (passage, privacy, keyed entry or dummy), its backset (2-3/8" or 2-3/4" in the US), its thickness (usually 1-3/8" interior or 1-3/4" exterior), and its handing if you are choosing a lever. Get those right and almost any brand will fit in 15 to 30 minutes using the existing holes. This guide walks you through each choice, then shows you exactly how to install and replace the hardware yourself.

Knobs vs. Levers: Which Should You Choose?

Both mount the same way and use the same holes. The difference is how people use them.

For most homes, levers are the better everyday choice, especially for anyone with arthritis or limited grip strength. Keep knobs where you want to slow down small children, such as on doors to garages, basements or laundry rooms.

The Four Door Hardware Functions

Passage

Turns from both sides and never locks. Use it for hallways, closets, pantries and any door that should always open.

Privacy

Locks from the inside with a push button or turn button. The outside has a small emergency release hole that opens with a pin or a flat tool. Use it for bedrooms and bathrooms. If you are childproofing, see how to get child-proof door knobs off without damaging the door.

Keyed Entry

Locks with a key from the outside and a thumb turn or button from the inside. Use it on exterior doors, always paired with a separate deadbolt for real security. Keyed knobs are not a substitute for a deadbolt because the latch is spring-loaded and can be slipped.

Dummy (Single or Pair)

A fixed handle with no latch. Use it on closet doors held shut with a ball catch or roller catch, or as the inactive side of a set of double doors.

Security Grades: What ANSI/BHMA Ratings Mean

Door hardware sold in North America is tested to ANSI/BHMA A156.2 for knobs and levers. The grade tells you how much abuse it survived in the lab.

GradeCycle TestTypical Use
Grade 11,000,000 cyclesCommercial and high-traffic doors
Grade 2400,000 cyclesQuality residential and light commercial
Grade 3200,000 cyclesBasic residential interior doors

For interior passage and privacy doors, Grade 3 is fine. For a front door, choose Grade 2 or better and add a Grade 1 or Grade 2 deadbolt. Not sure what you need? Try our Lock Security Matcher tool.

Finishes: Which Ones Last

  • PVD (physical vapor deposition): the toughest finish. Resists scratches and corrosion, and is often backed by a lifetime warranty. Worth it on exterior doors.
  • Satin nickel and satin chrome: durable, hides fingerprints, and matches most modern interiors.
  • Matte black: popular, but powder-coated versions can chip on high-use doors. Look for PVD black if possible.
  • Oil-rubbed bronze: a "living" finish that wears to show brass highlights over time. Some people love this; others see it as wear.
  • Lacquered polished brass: looks great at first, but the clear coat eventually wears and the brass tarnishes unevenly.

How to Install a Door Knob or Lever (Existing Holes)

Tools: Phillips screwdriver, tape measure and, if the latch plate needs adjusting, a sharp 1" chisel. Time: 15 to 30 minutes.

Step 1: Confirm the Backset

Most new latches adjust between 2-3/8" and 2-3/4". Check the latch you bought and set it to match your door before you start. Many latches adjust by rotating or sliding the faceplate.

Step 2: Install the Latch

Push the latch into the edge hole with the angled side of the bolt facing the direction the door closes. Seat a faceplate latch with its two screws, or tap a drive-in latch into place with a block of wood and a hammer.

Step 3: Fit the Exterior Side

Slide the outside knob or lever through the latch so its spindle and mounting posts pass through the holes in the latch mechanism. Keyed sides always go on the outside.

Step 4: Fit the Interior Side and Screw It Together

Line up the interior side, insert the two long through-bolts and tighten them evenly. Stop when the handle feels firm. Overtightening can bind the latch so it will not retract fully. If that happens, see our fix for a latch not retracting fully.

Step 5: Install or Adjust the Strike Plate

Close the door slowly and watch where the latch hits the frame. If it misses the strike hole, move the strike plate or file the opening slightly. On exterior doors, replace the short strike screws with 3" screws that reach the wall stud.

Step 6: Test From Both Sides

Turn the handle from each side, test the lock function, and close the door several times. The latch should click in without you having to push or lift the door.

How to Replace an Old Knob or Handle

  1. Remove the interior side. Look for two visible screws on the rose (the round plate). If there are none, look for a small slot or pin hole on the neck of the knob, press it with a small screwdriver and pull the knob off, then pop off the decorative rose to reveal the screws.
  2. Pull out both halves. Once the screws are out, the exterior side slides out.
  3. Remove the latch. Unscrew the faceplate on the door edge and pull the latch out.
  4. Measure before you shop. Check the backset, door thickness and bore size from the table above. If the new hardware has a larger rose, it will hide old marks.
  5. Install the new set using the steps above.

Special Cases

  • Thin doors (under 1-3/8"): standard knobs may not tighten. See what to do when a door is too thin for a knob.
  • Mobile homes: many mobile home doors use smaller backsets and thinner slabs. Read are mobile home door knobs different before buying.
  • Australia: handles are typically mounted higher than in the US. Here is why Australian door knobs are so high.
  • Double doors: use a passage or keyed set on the active door and a dummy or flush bolts on the inactive door. Our guide on how to lock a double door covers the options.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Buying the wrong backset. Measure first, even if the box says "adjustable."
  • Installing the latch upside down. The angled side must face the door stop, or the door will not latch.
  • Overtightening the through-bolts. This binds the mechanism and causes sticking handles.
  • Using a keyed knob as your only exterior lock. Always add a deadbolt.
  • Ignoring handing on levers. A non-reversible lever installed on the wrong door will point the wrong way or droop.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are all door knobs the same size?

Most US residential hardware fits a 2-1/8" bore hole with a 2-3/8" or 2-3/4" backset, and most new latches adjust between the two. Older homes, mobile homes and imported doors can differ, so measure before buying.

Can I replace a knob with a lever without drilling?

Usually yes. Levers use the same bore hole and latch hole as knobs. Check that the lever rose is large enough to cover any marks left by the old knob.

Do door knobs come with a strike plate?

Almost always. New sets include a latch, strike plate and screws. See do door knobs come with strike plates for what is included.

How long do door handles last?

A Grade 3 interior set typically lasts 10 to 20 years in normal use. Grade 2 and Grade 1 hardware can last decades. Finish wear usually shows before mechanical failure.

Do I need a locksmith to install a door knob?

No. If the door already has standard holes, installing a knob or lever is a simple DIY job. A locksmith is worth calling for rekeying, high-security locks or drilling a new door.

What is the standard height for a door handle?

In the US, handles are usually mounted 34" to 48" above the floor, with 36" to 38" being most common. ADA guidance places operable hardware between 34" and 48".

The Bottom Line

Measure the backset, door thickness and bore size, pick the right function for each room, and choose a lever unless you have a reason to slow children down. For exterior doors, buy Grade 2 or better with a PVD finish and pair it with a deadbolt. With the right parts in hand, installation takes less than half an hour with a single screwdriver.
