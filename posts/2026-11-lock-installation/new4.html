To install a mortise lock, you cut a deep rectangular pocket into the door edge to hold the lock body, drill holes through the door face for the cylinder and spindle, fit the lock body, trim and cylinder, then cut a matching strike into the frame. It is more work than a standard bored lock, usually 2 to 3 hours on a wooden door, but the result is the strongest residential lock you can install. Here is how to do it accurately.

What Is a Mortise Lock?

A mortise lock is a lock body that sits inside a pocket (the "mortise") cut into the door edge. One box combines the latch and the deadbolt, operated by a handle, a thumb turn and a key cylinder. You find them on older homes, upscale entry doors and almost every commercial building because they are durable and hard to defeat.

Before You Start: Check the Door

  • Thickness: most mortise locks need a door at least 1-3/4" thick. Thinner doors are too weak once the pocket is cut.
  • Construction: the door must be solid wood or solid core at the lock location. Hollow-core doors cannot take a mortise lock.
  • Stile width: the vertical edge piece must be wide enough for the lock body plus the backset (commonly 2-1/2" or 2-3/4").
  • Handing: many mortise locks must be set for left- or right-hand doors. Check before installing.

Tools and Materials

  • Mortise lock with trim, cylinder and strike
  • Drill with spade or Forstner bits matching the lock body width (often 3/4" to 1")
  • Sharp chisels (1/2" and 1")
  • Mortising jig (optional but recommended)
  • Combination square, marking knife and pencil
  • Hole saws or bits for the cylinder and spindle holes (per the template)
  • Screwdriver and hammer

How to Install a Mortise Lock: Step-by-Step

Step 1: Mark the Lock Position

Hold the lock body against the door edge at the right height, usually with the handle about 36" to 38" from the floor. Mark the top and bottom of the case and the center line of the edge.

Step 2: Drill Out the Pocket

Using a bit as wide as the lock case, drill a row of overlapping holes along the center line to the depth of the lock body plus about 1/8". Keep the drill perfectly square to the edge. A mortising jig keeps the holes straight.

Step 3: Clean the Pocket With a Chisel

Chisel the walls of the pocket flat and square until the lock body slides in without forcing. Test-fit often.

Step 4: Mortise the Faceplate

With the lock body in place, trace around its faceplate, remove the body and chisel a shallow recess so the faceplate sits flush with the door edge.

Step 5: Drill the Face Holes

Tape the template to the door face, lined up with the edge marks. Drill the spindle hole, the cylinder hole and any thumb turn hole, drilling from both sides to prevent tear-out.

Step 6: Install the Lock Body and Cylinder

Slide the lock body into the pocket and screw the faceplate in. Thread the mortise cylinder into the lock body until the cam is in the correct position, then tighten the cylinder set screw on the lock edge.

Step 7: Fit the Trim, Spindle and Handles

Install the escutcheons or roses on both faces, insert the spindle and attach the handles. Check that the latch, deadbolt and handles all work before closing the door.

Step 8: Cut and Install the Strike

Mark where the latch and deadbolt meet the frame, chisel pockets for both, then mortise the strike plate flush. Secure it with long screws into the framing.

Retrofitting an Old Mortise Lock

If you are replacing an existing mortise lock, measure the old case height, depth, backset and faceplate size before shopping. Many manufacturers make retrofit models sized to fit common older pockets. If the new body is slightly larger, you can enlarge the pocket with a chisel. If it is smaller, a retrofit faceplate or filler plate covers the gap.

Converting a mortise door to a standard bored lock is also possible with a conversion kit or plate, though you give up some security.

Common Mistakes

  • Drilling the pocket at an angle. The lock body will bind or break through the door face.
  • Cutting the pocket too deep or wide. This weakens the door edge.
  • Wrong handing. The latch faces the wrong way and the door will not close.
  • Overtightening the cylinder. The cam will not reach the lock mechanism and the key will not operate the bolt.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a mortise lock more secure than a deadbolt?

A quality Grade 1 mortise lock is one of the most secure residential options because it combines a heavy latch and deadbolt in a solid steel case set deep in the door.

Can I install a mortise lock myself?

Yes, if you are comfortable with a drill and chisel. It takes more precision than a bored lock. A mortising jig makes it much easier.

How long does mortise lock installation take?

About 2 to 3 hours for a new installation on a wooden door, or under an hour for a like-for-like replacement.

Can a mortise lock go on a hollow-core door?

No. Mortise locks need a solid door at least 1-3/4" thick.

What is a mortise cylinder?

A threaded cylinder that screws into the mortise lock body and is held by a set screw. It comes in lengths to suit different door and trim thicknesses.

The Bottom Line

A mortise lock rewards careful work with excellent security and decades of service. Check that your door is solid and thick enough, cut the pocket straight with a jig, mortise everything flush and set the cylinder depth correctly.
