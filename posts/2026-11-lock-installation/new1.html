To install a deadbolt on a wooden door, you mark the center line with the paper template, drill a 2-1/8" face hole and a 1" edge hole, chisel a shallow mortise for the bolt plate, fit the bolt and cylinders, then install a strike plate secured with 3" screws into the wall stud. With a $20 lock installation kit and a drill, most people finish in about an hour. Here is every step, plus the measurements that matter.

Tools and Materials

  • Deadbolt (Grade 2 or Grade 1 for exterior doors)
  • Lock installation kit: 2-1/8" hole saw and 1" spade or Forstner bit with a jig
  • Drill/driver
  • Sharp 1" wood chisel and hammer
  • Utility knife, pencil, tape measure and square
  • 3" wood screws for the strike plate (often not included)
  • Safety glasses

Single vs. Double Cylinder: Which Should You Buy?

Single cylinder deadbolts have a key outside and a thumb turn inside. They are the right choice for most homes because you can exit quickly in an emergency. Double cylinder deadbolts need a key on both sides. They help where glass sits within reach of the lock, but many local codes restrict them on bedroom exits and primary egress doors because they slow escape in a fire. Check your local rules before choosing one.

How to Install a Deadbolt: Step-by-Step

Step 1: Choose the Height

Place the deadbolt about 6" above the existing knob or handle. This keeps the bolt clear of the latch hardware and spreads force across more of the door. Avoid drilling where the door has a joint between the stile and a rail.

Step 2: Mark the Holes With the Template

Fold the paper template over the door edge and tape it in place. Choose the backset that matches the deadbolt (2-3/8" is common on interior-style doors, 2-3/4" on entry doors). Mark the center of the face hole on the door face and the center of the edge hole on the door edge. Use a square to carry the line across both faces if you are not using a jig.

Step 3: Drill the 2-1/8" Face Hole

Drill with the hole saw kept level until the pilot bit pokes through the far side. Stop, move to the other face and finish from there. Drilling from both sides prevents the veneer from splintering around the hole.

Step 4: Drill the 1" Edge Hole

Keep the bit square to the door edge and drill until you break into the face hole. A drilling jig makes this foolproof. If the bit wanders, the bolt will bind.

Step 5: Mortise the Bolt Faceplate

Insert the bolt into the edge hole, hold the faceplate square and trace around it with a utility knife. Remove the bolt and chisel out the outline to the depth of the faceplate, usually about 1/8", so it sits flush with the door edge. Some deadbolts have a round drive-in faceplate that needs no chiseling.

Step 6: Install the Bolt

Slide the bolt in with the word "UP" (or the arrow) facing up, then fasten the faceplate with the two short screws. Make sure the bolt is retracted before fitting the cylinders.

Step 7: Fit the Exterior Cylinder and Interior Thumb Turn

Pass the exterior cylinder's tailpiece through the bolt mechanism with the keyway pointing down. Fit the interior thumb turn over the tailpiece, line up the screw holes and tighten the two through-bolts evenly. Test the thumb turn and key several times before moving on. The bolt should throw and retract smoothly.

Step 8: Install the Strike Plate and Reinforce It

Rub lipstick or chalk on the end of the bolt, close the door and turn the bolt so it marks the jamb. Drill a 1" hole about 1-1/4" deep at the mark, then mortise the strike plate flush into the jamb. Fasten it with 3" screws that reach the wall framing. This is the single biggest security upgrade you can make: short strike screws are how most doors get kicked in.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Drilling from one side only. This tears out the far face of the door.
  • Using the short screws supplied for the strike. Swap them for 3" screws on any exterior door.
  • Installing the bolt upside down. The bolt may not throw fully or may jam.
  • A strike hole that is too shallow. If the bolt cannot fully extend, it is not locked and an attacker can push it back.
  • Overtightening the through-bolts. This binds the mechanism and makes the key hard to turn.

How Secure Is Your Deadbolt?

Look for an ANSI/BHMA grade on the packaging. Grade 1 is commercial strength, Grade 2 is a strong residential choice and Grade 3 is basic. For front and back doors, choose Grade 2 or better. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I install a deadbolt myself?

Yes. With a lock installation kit and a drill, installing a deadbolt is a straightforward DIY job that takes about an hour on a wooden door.

How far above the door knob should a deadbolt be?

About 6" above the knob, measured center to center. This keeps the hardware from interfering and adds strength.

What size hole do I need for a deadbolt?

A 2-1/8" hole through the door face and a 1" hole through the door edge, with a 2-3/8" or 2-3/4" backset.

Do I need a strike plate for a deadbolt?

Yes. The strike plate protects the jamb and, when secured with 3" screws into the stud, is what stops the door from being kicked open.

Can I add a deadbolt to a hollow-core door?

Hollow-core doors usually have solid wood blocking only around the knob. A deadbolt needs solid material, so it is better to replace the door with a solid-core or exterior-rated door.

Should I rekey the deadbolt to match my other locks?

It is convenient. Many brands sell deadbolts and handlesets "keyed alike," or a locksmith can rekey them to one key for a small fee.

The Bottom Line

Installing a deadbolt is one of the best security upgrades for any wooden door. Measure carefully, drill from both sides, mortise the faceplates flush, and above all secure the strike plate with 3" screws into the framing. Done right, your deadbolt will lock smoothly and stand up to real-world force.
